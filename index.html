2025 and 2026 Student AI Surveys

How often students use AI for academic work

This section opens after the prediction at the top of the page.

Go to the prediction or .

Weekly or more frequent use rose from 36.6% in 2025 to 50.3% in 2026. Most of that growth came from the middle: use that was monthly or less often than weekly fell from 40.5% to 29.1%. The share who never use AI for academic work moved less, from 23.0% to 20.6%.

For your course

Plan for a mixed room. In a class of 30, expect about 15 students who use AI weekly or more and about 6 who never use it for academic work. Directions that assume everyone uses AI, or that no one does, will miss part of the class. Try your own class size in Picture your class.

Say what you expect for each assignment rather than leaving students to infer it from your general stance.

Research context

Reading the trend. In Rogers' diffusion of innovations model (2003), adoption passing 50% marks the move from the early majority to the late majority. The categories describe how a practice spreads, not students' character. Some non-users hold considered ethical objections (Jensen, 2026), and researchers have proposed "non-adopters" in place of "laggards" (Stewart et al., 2019).

    Next: How students use AI in coursework

    2025 and 2026 Student AI Surveys

    How students use AI in coursework

    In 2025, two answers tied at 48.1%: working on assignments without consulting AI, and consulting AI for ideas before writing. In 2026 the tie broke. Consulting AI for ideas rose to 49.3% and working without AI fell to 41.4%. The workflows that hand more of the writing to AI barely moved.

    For your course

    The most common uses happen before anything is submitted: generating ideas and getting feedback. If the early thinking is what you want students to learn, build those steps into the assignment and give credit for them, such as a planning note, a first draft, or a short account of how the idea developed.

    Research context

    Why the early steps matter. Effort that feels slow often produces more durable learning (Bjork, 1994). In a field experiment, students with unrestricted GPT-4 access did better on practice problems but 17% worse once access was removed; a version designed to give hints instead of answers largely avoided the drop (Bastani et al., 2025). A lab study found ChatGPT improved essay scores without improving knowledge gain or transfer (Fan et al., 2025).

    Next: Where students draw the line

    2025 and 2026 Student AI Surveys

    Where students draw the line

    Large majorities call it cheating to rely on AI to write an entire assignment (91.5%) or to submit AI work without acknowledgment (81.4%). Both shares dipped slightly from 2025. More students now place the line at the instructor's rules: using AI without permission rose from 54.2% to 58.6%. About a quarter see uncredited brainstorming help as cheating.

    How well students understand the policy

    Ratings of excellent or very good rose from 30.0% to 33.5%, and poor fell from 13.2% to 10.1%. The middle held: 56.4% still rate their understanding good or fair.

    For your course

    Students increasingly look to the instructor for the rule, and most are only moderately sure of the policy. State expectations at the assignment level, and say whether and how to acknowledge AI help with ideas, since three in four students do not see uncredited brainstorming as cheating.

    Next: What students worry about

    2025 and 2026 Student AI Surveys

    What students worry about

    Concern about a decline in critical thinking held at 87.0%, the highest of eleven items in both years. Concerns about students' own skills held steady or rose. Concerns about AI's wider effects rose, led by energy and environmental impact (up 11.5 points, the largest change in the survey). Concerns about AI's reliability, ethics, and policy fell.

    Who is more concerned (2026)

    Students who rarely or never use AI reported more concern than frequent users on all eleven items. Women reported more concern than men on most items. Use the tabs to switch comparisons.

    For your course

    Students share the worry many faculty hold. Name critical thinking as a goal and show students where an assignment builds it; most will recognize the reason.

    Concern differs by use. Students who rarely use AI may want a way to complete work without it. Frequent users, who report less concern about bias and privacy, may gain the most from tasks that test AI output.

    Research context

    On the critical thinking worry. A survey of 666 participants found heavier AI use associated with lower critical thinking scores, partly through cognitive offloading, with the strongest pattern among people aged 17 to 25 (Gerlich, 2025). The design is correlational, so it cannot show that AI caused the difference. It does support designing tasks that keep the reasoning with the student.

    In this study, heavy users use AI daily or more; casual users use it more than monthly but not daily. Both samples are self-selected.

    1Who uses AI, by school or college and class year

    Heavy use ranged from 36% of Arts and Sciences respondents to 63% in the Opus College of Business. Non-use was highest in Arts and Sciences (16%). Third- and fourth-year students reported more heavy use than first- and second-year students (51% vs. 43%).

    2How they use it for school

    AI users describe AI more as a tutor than a substitute. Just over half (53%) say it mostly helps them understand steps or explanations; 1% say it mostly writes their work.

    3What worries them

    Their top concern is becoming dependent on AI. Casual users worry more about environmental impact (48% vs. 30%) and are about twice as likely to always double-check AI answers against other sources (27% vs. 14%).

    4Who they ask about AI

    Professors ranked fourth. Students turn first to their own reflection, friends, and online sources. Heavy users were less likely than casual users to ask professors (23% vs. 31%).

    The faculty stances students described

    In listening sessions, students described faculty stances on a range from forbidding to integrating, and said that variation from course to course leaves them unsure what is fair.

    Forbidding

    No role for AI; often uses detectors, blue books, or handwritten work.

    Indifferent

    Little or no guidance; unclear whether use would be questioned.

    Guiding

    Suggests appropriate uses and draws clear lines where AI is not allowed.

    Integrating

    Builds AI into coursework and discusses the tradeoffs openly.

    5What they want from St. Thomas

    AI users most want to see how AI is used in their field (37%). Non-users most want guidance on ethical use (42%) and chances to discuss AI's effect on society (34%).

    How AI use relates to students' values

    When asked how their AI use relates to their values, 41% of AI users said they had not thought about it yet; 30% said it is sometimes aligned and sometimes not; 14% said it is often in tension.

    6In their words

    Four comments from the listening sessions show both the value students see and the limits they set for themselves.

    • "It's like having an on-demand instructor. Sometimes you can't reach out to learn, it can stand in and help when you need it."

      AI user, listening session

    • "If I've got an assignment due and have procrastinated, or I'm in a time crunch, those boundaries can go away."

      AI user, listening session

    • "I'm studying to become a nurse. I can't use AI to avoid actually learning, or someone could get hurt."

      AI user, listening session

    • "With a professor, I am not always comfortable telling them they didn't understand my question... or that I didn't understand their response."

      AI user, listening session

    For your course

    Students set their own rules, and those rules give way under time pressure. They want course-level rules with reasons, consistency, and a safe way to ask. Say in class that questions about AI are welcome and will be treated as questions. Explain what each assignment is meant to build, so the reason for a limit is visible when deadlines press.

    Next: Principles and five levels of AI use

    From evidence to course design

    These tools follow an AI-aware approach: start from what an assignment is for, then decide AI's role, whether that means using AI, setting firm limits, or something between (Vee et al., 2026). They build on six principles.

    • Start with the learning goal

      Decide what students must be able to do, then choose AI's role. The goal, not the tool, sets the rule (Vee et al., 2026; Bowen & Watson, 2025).

    • Make purpose, task, and criteria explicit

      Transparent assignment design improved students' confidence, belonging, and skill development, with larger gains for first-generation, low-income, and underrepresented students (Winkelmes et al., 2016).

    • Use a scale, not a yes or no

      Levels of permitted AI use give students and instructors a shared vocabulary (Perkins et al., 2024).

    • Change the structure, not only the rules

      A rule students cannot be seen to follow does not secure an assessment. Build validity into the task itself, through observed work, process evidence, or conversation (Corbin et al., 2025; Lodge et al., 2023).

    • Keep the productive struggle

      Protect the effortful steps that build a skill, and use AI where it removes effort that does not teach (Bjork, 1994; Bastani et al., 2025).

    • Offer more than one path

      Some students choose not to use AI for ethical reasons. Provide ways to reach the same goal with and without it (CAST, 2024; Jensen, 2026).

    Five levels of AI use

    Both tools use this scale, adapted from the AI Assessment Scale (Perkins et al., 2024). A course can use different levels for different assignments.

    1. No AIWork completed without AI tools.
    2. AI to learn, not to produceExplanations, quizzing, starting points; no AI output in the submission.
    3. AI feedback on your own draftStudent writes first; AI critiques; student decides.
    4. AI as a disclosed collaboratorAI generates parts; student directs, verifies, revises, documents.
    5. AI as the subject of studyStudents test and critique AI output.

    Next: Discussion guides

    Discussion guides

    Three ready-to-run formats for faculty learning communities, department meetings, the AI Practitioners Group, or your own class. Each prints on its own.

    Read the room together

    Time: 40 minutesGroup: 4 to 20 facultyBring: this page on a shared screen; a list of your program's major assignments

    Goal: agree on one change that makes AI expectations more consistent for students across your program.

    1. 5 min. Everyone writes down three guesses: the share of students who use AI weekly or more, the share who work on assignments without AI, and the share worried about losing critical thinking skills. Compare guesses before revealing the answers on this page.
    2. 10 min. Look at the answers. Ask: which result surprised us most, and what shaped our expectation? Which results matter most for our discipline?
    3. 15 min. List three to five major assignments in the program. Place each on the five-level scale. Where would a student taking two of our courses in the same term get different messages? Students in the spring 2026 study described that inconsistency as a source of anxiety about fairness.
    4. 10 min. Choose one shared commitment, such as common syllabus language, a common way to acknowledge AI use, or a shared explanation of why certain skills are practiced without AI. Name who will draft it and when.

    Questions to keep in view

    • Our students rank clear guidelines first. Could a student state our program's expectations?
    • About one in five students never uses AI for academic work. Do any of our assignments require it without offering another path?

    Redesign one assignment

    Time: 60 minutesGroup: pairs or trios within a larger groupBring: one current assignment each

    Goal: leave with a revised assignment whose purpose, AI rules, and criteria a student could restate.

    1. 10 min. Individually, write one sentence on what the assignment is for. Then answer: which step would a student most likely hand to AI, and is that the step where the learning happens?
    2. 10 min. Run the assignment through the planner. Compare the suggested level with your current rule. Note any gap.
    3. 15 min. Make one structural change, not only a rule change: an in-class step, a draft checkpoint, a short oral explanation, a revision note, or a comparison with AI output (Corbin et al., 2025).
    4. 15 min. Rewrite the directions with a purpose, the task steps, the criteria, and the AI rule with its reason (Winkelmes et al., 2016). Trade with a partner. Can your partner restate what is allowed and why?
    5. 10 min. Share one change with the group. Agree to try it and report back at the next meeting on what students did and said.

    Questions to keep in view

    • Only 1.4% of students report submitting AI output unedited, but 49.3% use AI for ideas. Which of those two matters more for this assignment?
    • What would a student who does not use AI do at each step?

    Ask your students

    Time: 20 minutes of classGroup: any class sizeBring: index cards or an anonymous poll

    Goal: hear how your students use AI in your course and agree on norms they understand. Students in the spring 2026 study said they want to ask about AI without being treated as suspects, and only 27% of AI users turn to professors now.

    1. 3 min. Explain why you are asking: you want the course's AI rules to support their learning, and you will use what they say.
    2. 5 min. Share two St. Thomas findings: 87% of students worry AI could weaken critical thinking, and half use AI weekly or more. Ask students to write anonymously: where in this course could AI help you learn, and where could it get in the way?
    3. 7 min. Read a sample of cards aloud. Connect what students wrote to the purpose of one upcoming assignment.
    4. 5 min. State the AI expectations for that assignment and the reason for them. Invite follow-up questions in office hours or by email, and say that asking will never count against them.

    After class

    • Adjust one assignment direction based on what you read, and tell students what you changed.
    • Note which uses students named that you had not anticipated.

    Methods notes

    • Two-year survey figures match the AERA proposal tables. Participants who answered fewer than half of the non-demographic questions were excluded. Item-level n varies slightly with item nonresponse.
    • Year-over-year changes are differences of the published one-decimal values.
    • Concern items were coded in opposite directions in the two years and harmonized so that 5 means most concerned. The 2025 data privacy item was worded as an ethical concern and is mapped to the 2026 data privacy item, following the AERA analysis.
    • Group comparisons of concern used Mann-Whitney U tests on 2026 data, with Bonferroni-corrected alpha of .0045 and effect size r (.1 small, .3 medium; Cohen, 1988). Average concern is shown reversed from the survey's coding so that higher values mean more concern.
    • The 2025 and 2026 questions on preferred training formats used single-select and multi-select formats and are not compared here.
    • Spring 2026 study figures are reproduced from the published report; the study's statistical tests compared heavy and casual users at 95% confidence. Results by gender from that study are not shown pending verification of the report's table.
    • All three sources rely on voluntary participation and self-report, so results may not represent all students.
